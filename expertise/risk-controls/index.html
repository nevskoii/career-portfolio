Risk & Controls

How I approached enterprise risk, control design and process reviews in a data centre business.

Rostelecom (Data Centres)Lead Risk and Internal Control Specialist2023–2026

In my role at Rostelecom (Data Centres), I worked across financial, commercial, operational and IT processes. My responsibility was to connect business risks to specific controls, assess how those controls operated, and work with process owners to resolve identified weaknesses.

Understanding the control landscape

Before testing individual controls, it helps to understand where they sit within the business. I structured control areas around two perspectives: general business processes and the operating environment of data centres.

The diagrams illustrate control practices and review areas rather than reproducing confidential company policies or control registers.

These maps group examples of preventive, detective and corrective control activities. They are illustrations of control concepts, not a statement that every listed standard or procedure was individually audited by me.

My role and the scope of work

I helped develop and maintain a risk and internal control framework covering approximately 90 risks and 250 controls across financial and operational processes. The work also supported operational risk governance, regulatory reporting and IPO readiness.

I delivered 10+ end-to-end risk-based business process reviews and took part in risk discussions with management and process owners. The objective was not simply to maintain a risk register, but to understand whether risks were appropriately addressed by controls that could actually be executed and evidenced.

How I approached a control review

  1. Understand the process. Identify business objectives, process owners, key transactions, systems and decision points.
  2. Assess the risk. Consider what could go wrong, the potential operational or financial consequences and existing mitigations.
  3. Map the controls. Document control objectives, responsibilities, frequency, required evidence and preventive or detective mechanisms.
  4. Test and discuss. Conduct walkthroughs, review selected records, investigate exceptions and agree findings with the responsible teams.
  5. Follow up. Document Corrective Action Plans (CAPs), track implementation and report material risks and remediation status.

The controls I worked with

Some of the most useful control assessments involved reconciling what a procedure required with what had actually happened in the system, in a contract or in the operational record.

Examples of findings and business impact

Revenue assurance

I identified colocation discounts exceeding approved limits by up to 30%, as well as unauthorised long-term free rack reservations. These findings triggered reviews of customer contracts, pricing and revenue controls.

Contract and operating cost review

I built an Excel comparison of contracted and actual power consumption across approximately 4,000 racks. The analysis identified electricity charges up to 4.5% above contractual terms and prompted reviews of 42 counterparty agreements.

From process maps to a risk–control matrix

The process maps show where controls belong; a risk–control matrix (RCM) makes each control testable. I used matrices to connect risks with control objectives, owners, execution frequency, supporting evidence and review procedures. This structure supported risk self-assessments, control testing and follow-up on exceptions.

What I learned from this work

The quality of a control depends on more than its documented description. It needs a clear owner, evidence of execution and a practical response when exceptions arise. Working across financial and technical processes reinforced the importance of combining process knowledge, analytical work and conversations with the people responsible for day-to-day operations.
