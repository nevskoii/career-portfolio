Project 01 · Internal control system

Creating an internal control system: from business process to control evidence

The point of an internal control system is not to maintain a large register. It is to connect business objectives, process risks, control actions, accountable owners and evidence into one management system.

A risk-oriented approach starts with the process. Each process has a business objective, a set of risks that may prevent that objective from being achieved, and a set of controls that either prevent the risk, detect it after the event, or correct the consequences.

How the risk-oriented logic works

The same structure can be applied to procurement, finance, revenue, treasury, fixed assets, IT access or risk governance:

01PROCESSWhat business activity creates value or exposure?
02RISKWhat can go wrong and what would the consequence be?
03CONTROLWhat prevents, detects or corrects the risk?
04OWNERWho performs and who reviews the control?
05EVIDENCEWhat record proves that the control operated?
06FOLLOW-UPHow are exceptions resolved and tracked?

This is where control design becomes more than a policy statement. A useful control description answers: who performs it, when it is triggered, what data is checked, what happens if the result is satisfactory, what happens if an exception is found, and what evidence remains.

Applying the same approach to data-centre operations

Data centres have a pronounced IT and infrastructure profile. Commercial commitments, availability, power capacity, cooling, physical security, access rights, incident management, maintenance and vendors all interact with each other. A failure in one area can quickly become a service, financial or contractual risk.

That is why a data-centre control system needs both business controls and technical-operational controls. The business side covers approvals, contracts, billing, cost allocation and governance. The operational side covers infrastructure monitoring, change management, incident response, BCP/DRP, access, suppliers and compliance with internal and external requirements.

Reference framework

The control environment can be mapped to several layers: enterprise risk management, information security, business continuity, IT service management, infrastructure standards, internal policies, SLAs and contract obligations. In practice, the task is to translate those requirements into operationally executable controls.

Risk governanceRisk taxonomy, risk appetite, owners, KRIs, RCSA and management reporting.
Information securityAccess management, privileged accounts, logging, change control and segregation of duties.
Business continuityBCP/DRP, backup checks, recovery testing, incident escalation and lessons learned.
Service managementSLA monitoring, incident and problem management, change windows and service reporting.
Infrastructure resiliencePower, cooling, monitoring, redundancy, maintenance and capacity utilisation.
Contracts and financePricing, billing, cost allocation, counterparty terms, approvals and evidence retention.

Risk–Control Matrix as the operating layer

A Risk–Control Matrix turns the framework into a working management tool. It links each risk to a control objective, the exact execution procedure, accountable roles, frequency, system dependence, evidence, testing results and remediation actions.

The example below shows the level of detail expected from a complete control. It is intentionally written as an operational algorithm rather than a generic policy line.

Detailed control example · EX-CP-004

Monthly reconciliation of billing data to the financial ledger

Risk: billing data may be omitted, duplicated or inaccurately reflected in accounting records.

Who
Revenue Accountant; review overseen by the Revenue Accounting Manager.
When
Monthly, after the billing interface run and before the reporting period is closed.
What
Compare billing exports and ERP revenue records by service category and reporting period; investigate unmatched or unusual items.
If the check passes
Record a nil-exception reconciliation, retain source reports and obtain reviewer confirmation.
If it fails
Consult the billing process owner, initiate an authorised correction, repeat the comparison and document any outstanding difference or escalation.
Result
A reviewed billing-to-ledger reconciliation with a documented disposition of exceptions.
Control trace
Dated billing export, ERP report, reconciliation worksheet, exception correspondence and sign-off.

Project 02 · Management control dashboards

Dashboards as a management control tool

The dashboard project strengthened management control by turning fragmented operational and commercial data into regular, repeatable reporting for technical and sales teams.

Instead of manually reviewing separate extracts, managers could see capacity utilisation, power load and sold commitments in one interactive hierarchy — from the data-centre level down to individual server halls.

STAR summary

ElementDescription
SituationCapacity, sales and consumption data were difficult to monitor through static extracts and disconnected reports. Technical teams needed visibility over load and spare capacity; sales teams needed a clearer view of sold commitments and commercial inventory.
TaskCreate a management reporting layer that would reduce manual analytical work, make exceptions visible, and support decisions on capacity allocation, sales commitments and electricity-cost review.
ActionStructured data into a hierarchy of 27 data centres and 113 server halls; prepared capacity, utilisation and sales metrics; and built interactive treemap dashboards using Python, Pandas, SQLite and Plotly.
ResultThe dashboards created a repeatable reporting basis for technical and commercial review: projected capacity of 130 MW, actual load of 78 MW, average utilisation of 60%, and sales commitments relative to capacity. Manual analysis time was reduced by approximately 50%, while capacity pressure, underutilised inventory and potential overselling became easier to identify.

What improved

Less manual workRoutine capacity and sales analysis moved from scattered extracts to a structured dashboard view, reducing manual preparation and review time.
Regular management reportingTechnical and sales teams received a common visual basis for reviewing power load, sold capacity, spare capacity and exceptions.
Capacity allocation controlThe treemap hierarchy made it easier to see where load was concentrated, where spare capacity remained, and where sales activity needed to be aligned with operational limits.
Electricity-cost reviewCombining consumption data with contractual and operational context strengthened the review of power charges and helped identify potential overpayments or tariff deviations.
Sales-risk visibilitySold capacity could be compared against physical capacity, making potential overselling or underutilisation visible earlier.
Better evidence for follow-upDashboard outputs provided a clearer basis for management discussions, exception follow-up and corrective action tracking.

Why this matters for controls

The value of the dashboards is not only analytical. They create a repeatable management-control mechanism: data is consolidated, thresholds are visible, exceptions are highlighted and responsible teams can act on the same evidence base.

In control terms, the dashboards support a detective management control: they do not prevent a capacity issue from arising, but they make deviations visible early enough for commercial and operational owners to review and respond.

The connecting idea

The internal control system defines what should be controlled and why. The RCM defines who performs each control, when and with what evidence. Dashboards then strengthen management control by making exceptions visible, repeatable and traceable.

Together, these elements turn risk management from a static register into an operating discipline: process → risk → control → evidence → action.
